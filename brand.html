Oels

Oels is the company. Anteroom is the product — the room where someone waits before being admitted. Every decision below serves two readers at once: a London solicitor deciding whether this vendor is serious, and a Gulf enterprise buyer recognising a visual language that belongs to them.

01 · The bridge

The aperture

The mark was an arch when the company was called Anteroom, because an arch is structurally already the letter A. Renaming the company to Oels closed the arch into an aperture — an O that is still a threshold you can see through, carrying the same brass inner line and the same horizontal bar across it. The concept did not move: a doorway that filters what passes through, which is a near-literal description of the product. Only the letter did. Brass does the same work as before — the nameplate outside a chambers, and the gold that runs through Gulf premium branding.

02 · Colour

Najd green, brass, limestone

The shift from blue-slate to a deep green-black is where the heritage enters. Green is the colour of the palm and the oasis, and it runs through Najdi and wider Gulf visual tradition — but this is a deep, desaturated green, deliberately not the Saudi flag green (#006C35). That flag carries the shahada; a private company appropriating it reads as presumptuous rather than respectful. Limestone replaces the cool off-white, drawn from the mud-brick and stone of Diriyah, warmed just enough to feel Gulf without drifting into the terracotta-on-cream that every AI design tool currently produces.

03 · The motif

Mashrabiya — the screen at the threshold

The mashrabiya is the carved lattice screen set in a doorway or window: it filters what passes through while still letting light and air in, and it lets the person inside see out without being seen. That is a near-literal description of what this product does at a firm's front door. It is the rare case where a heritage motif is also an accurate product metaphor rather than decoration — which is exactly the test any ornament has to pass here.

Live · enquiry in progress
The screen frames the transcript. Used at low opacity as texture behind dark sections, and as the border treatment around the live-demo panel — never as loud wallpaper.

Rules: the lattice appears at 10–18% opacity as texture only, or as a hairline frame. It is never a full-strength background, never coloured beyond brass, and never combined with a second decorative pattern. One motif, held with discipline, reads as confidence — two reads as a theme.

04 · Typography

Caslon, Plex, Plex Mono — unchanged

Deliberately left alone. Caslon is 18th-century London, the letterform of legal printing, and it carries authority in both markets without costume. Resist the pull toward a decorative Arabic-inspired Latin display face — that is the single fastest way to make a heritage-aware brand look like a themed restaurant. If Arabic-language pages are added later, pair with a proper Arabic typeface (IBM Plex Sans Arabic already matches the body face) rather than styling Latin type to imitate Arabic.

One action colour, four real states

06 · Coherence rules

How this holds together

BrassOnly ever means "this is the action" or "this is live". Never decorative fill. Brass Deep for text on limestone, Brass Light on dark.
Deep GreenReserved for feature sections and the demo panel. In light mode it must stay rare, or it stops signalling "this is the live product".

Corners3–4px throughout. Not pills, not squares.
Themedata-theme on <html>, defaulting to prefers-color-scheme. React state only — never localStorage.

The tension worth naming: the immediate buyer is a UK solicitor or adviser, and the Middle East market comes later. This palette is built so the heritage reads as quietly architectural and premium to the first audience and as familiar to the second — deep green and brass are at home in Lincoln's Inn and in Riyadh alike. If a decision ever forces a choice between the two, serve the buyer you have now. Heritage that costs you your first client is expensive decoration.
