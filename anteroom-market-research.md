# Anteroom — Market Research

Where the demand is, where the gaps are, and what the realistic revenue looks like. All figures dated 2025–2026 and sourced from regulators and published surveys rather than vendor marketing.

---

## The headline finding

**The gap is not AI adoption. Adoption already happened. The gap is that nobody has operationalised it.**

Nearly nine in ten UK and Ireland legal professionals now use AI in some capacity, with 70% having adopted within the past year. But only 29% of firms have implemented workflow automation, 53% say their firm has no AI policy or they are unaware of one, 54% provide no AI training, and 43% have no formal policy at all.

That is the entire business in two sentences. Individual solicitors are pasting things into ChatGPT. Their firms have no system, no governance, no audit trail and no automation. You are not selling AI to people who have never heard of it. You are selling *structure* to people who are already using AI badly and know it.

This is a much better position than selling novelty. The buyer already believes in the technology. What they lack is a way to use it that survives contact with their regulator.

**The firms have money right now.** The Law Society's 2026 benchmarking survey found median fee income up 11.2%, roughly double the previous year's 6.1%, described as the highest growth in more than fifteen years. Profitability per equity partner rose 10.5%. This matters enormously for timing: you are entering a market where your buyers have just had a good year.

**The pain is quantified and it is admin.** Four in ten legal professionals spend between two and five hours a day on administrative tasks, and 44% cite excessive manual administrative work as a primary cost barrier. That is your automation pitch, already written by the industry itself.

**The barriers are exactly your differentiators.** Confidentiality, data protection and regulatory compliance are repeatedly cited as the main barriers to wider adoption, and 77% of lawyers remain concerned about AI producing inaccurate outputs. Meanwhile 29% of UK lawyers fear their firm is falling behind competitors who have adopted. Fear of being left behind, plus fear of compliance risk, is the most sellable combination there is.

**The constraint to respect:** only 34% of small firms plan to invest over £100,000 in technology annually. Your £249–849 pricing sits comfortably inside what a small firm can approve without a committee, which is deliberate and correct.

**The structural opportunity here is bigger than legal.** The FCA estimates around 23 million adults are underserved, including 7 million with at least £10,000 in investable assets who could benefit from advice and are not getting it. Nearly a third of firms told the FCA they are exploring simplified advice propositions to reach them.

Those firms need a way to handle far more inbound enquiries without adding advisers, and to stay firmly on the guidance side of the advice boundary while doing it. That is precisely what you built. The advice gap is your automation pitch in finance, and it is a regulator-endorsed problem rather than one you have to convince anyone exists.

**Also note:** 87% of advice firms have five or fewer advisers, and firm numbers are falling while adviser numbers hold steady. Consolidation means fewer, slightly larger buyers, each with more budget. Good for you.

### UK SMEs generally — context for the wider automation offer

Active AI use among UK firms reached 54% in March 2026, up from 35% in 2025 and 25% in 2024. But only around 16% have made a strategic deployment with a defined business purpose, and roughly 80% have neither deployed nor planned to. Among small firms, 46% say they lack the knowledge to use AI, and 49% of non-adopters cite data privacy concerns. Only 12% of AI-using businesses report increased revenue so far.

Read that last figure carefully. Most businesses using AI are not making more money from it. Your pitch cannot be "adopt AI." It has to be "here is the specific process that costs you money, and here is it automated." Vague AI enablement is a saturated, unconvincing offer.

### London vs Romford vs the rest of the UK

**London.** 40% of all lawyers, the deepest market, and the most competition. Every AI vendor in Britain is selling into London. Realistic entry point is not the City but the outer boroughs and the small high-street firms, which have the same admin problem and none of the in-house IT.

**Romford.** Small in absolute terms. A borough like Havering supports a few dozen firms rather than a few hundred. But market size is the wrong metric for your first client. What Romford gives you is something London cannot: you can physically walk into a solicitor's office on the high street, ask for the practice manager, and be back for a follow-up on Thursday. Your first three clients should come from within an hour of where you live, because proximity converts and cold email does not. Treat Romford and the surrounding Essex and outer-east-London belt as your beachhead, not your market.

**Other UK cities worth a second wave**, roughly in order of firm density outside London: Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Bristol, Nottingham, Sheffield. All have substantial mid-market legal and advice sectors and materially less vendor noise than London. Leeds and Bristol in particular have strong professional-services clusters relative to their size.

## Saudi Arabia — Riyadh, Dammam, Khobar

### The single most important finding in this document

**Saudi data law makes your self-hosting offer close to mandatory rather than optional.**

The Personal Data Protection Law has been in full force since September 2023, with the grace period expiring in September 2024. The default expectation is that personal data of Saudi residents stays on Saudi infrastructure, and transferring it outward requires SDAIA adequacy designation or approved safeguards. For government entities the rule is absolute: cloud providers must ensure no public-sector data leaves Saudi Arabia for any purpose, even temporarily for caching or redundancy.

Enforcement is active. SDAIA can order non-compliant processing to stop and improperly collected data to be destroyed. Foreign organisations processing Saudi residents' data must appoint a representative inside the Kingdom.

The US government's own trade analysis notes this creates new demand for data residency solutions, local hosting infrastructure and hybrid cloud models.

Every international AI vendor selling a multi-tenant cloud product has a structural problem in Saudi Arabia. You do not, because you deploy per client on their infrastructure. This is the rare case where a small operator has a genuine structural advantage over a funded platform, and it is worth more than any feature.

### Market size and momentum

Estimates vary by methodology, which is normal for this kind of forecast. Take the range rather than any single number:

- Saudi AI market at **$2.14bn in 2025, projected to $16.9bn by 2032** at 34.3% CAGR (MarketsandMarkets)
- Alternatively **$5.2bn in 2025 to $14.3bn by 2032** at 15.8% (PS Market Research)
- Big data and AI specifically: **$0.68bn in 2026 to $2.81bn by 2031** at 32.87% (Mordor)

Underlying that: over $20bn invested in AI infrastructure and talent, a $40bn sovereign AI fund launched in 2024, government spending on emerging technologies up 56% in 2024 alone, and a target of SAR 74bn in AI sector GDP contribution by 2030.

### Where you actually fit

Ignore the headline billions. Those are data centres, chips and government megaprojects, and you are not competing for them. Your opportunity is in the stated weakness of the market:

Analysts consistently name **the varying AI readiness of traditional enterprises and SMEs, and limited local AI talent**, as the structural restraints on the Saudi market. The SAMAI programme aims to train a million citizens by 2030, but demand already triples supply, and competing employers bid salaries around 50% above Gulf averages.

Translated: there is enormous funded demand, a severe shortage of people who can actually implement, and a mass of traditional mid-sized companies who are being told to adopt AI and have no idea how. That is a consultancy market, not a product market, and consultancy is what you are.

### Riyadh vs the Eastern Province

**Riyadh** is where the government, SDAIA, the megaprojects and the head offices are. Deepest market, most competition, hardest to enter cold, and the place where relationships matter most.

**Dammam and Khobar** are the Eastern Province industrial and oil-services belt, which is where AMC operates and where Hammad's network actually is. This is the more sensible entry point by a wide margin. The businesses there are traditional mid-sized industrial and services firms, exactly the "varying AI readiness" cohort the analysts describe, and they are precisely the type of company that has been told to modernise without being told how.

**Your unfair advantage in Saudi is not the product. It is that your CEO spent a decade as CFO and board member at a Dammam industrial supplier with Aramco and SLB engagements.** In a market where business runs on personal relationship and trust, that is worth more than any website. Warm introductions from that network into Eastern Province mid-market firms is the single highest-value channel available to you anywhere in this document.

### What to know before selling there

- You will likely need a local entity or a Saudi partner. Foreign firms processing Saudi personal data need a representative inside the Kingdom.
- Registration with SDAIA and ongoing obligations including periodic audits.
- Sectoral regulators interpret the PDPL inconsistently, which forces legal review and slows deals.
- Deal sizes are larger and cycles are longer than the UK. Budget for that in cashflow.

## Malmö and Sweden

I am going to be blunt, because the numbers are not encouraging.

Sweden is genuinely strong on AI overall: 35% of Swedish enterprises use AI, third in the EU. But the country ranks comparatively low in international assessments of national capacity to leverage AI despite robust digital infrastructure, which is why the government set up an AI Commission in the first place.

**Malmö specifically is the weakest market in this entire document.** Venture funding in the twelve months to Q2 2026 was $115m in Malmö against $4.1bn in Stockholm, with 38 of Sweden's 58 unicorns in the capital. Only 15 funded AI startups are tracked in Malmö at all.

Malmö's genuine strengths are lower costs and a 35-minute train to Copenhagen. It is a good place to *live* and hire cheaply. It is not where the buyers are.

**My honest recommendation: drop Sweden unless you have a specific personal connection there.** You would be entering a foreign language market, under EU AI Act and Swedish data rules you do not know, with no network, no local entity and no reason for a Swedish firm to choose an unknown British supplier over a local one. Every hour spent on Malmö is an hour not spent on the two markets where you have a genuine structural advantage.

If there *is* a personal connection — family, a contact, a reason you named that city specifically — say so and I will look at it properly, because a warm introduction changes the calculation entirely.

## What the money actually looks like

Realistic scenarios, using the cashflow model's assumptions. These are not projections, they are arithmetic on stated inputs.

**UK reception only, conservative.** Your addressable market is roughly 8,000 small and mid-sized law firms plus about 5,500 advice firms. Assume only 10% are realistically reachable and interested, which is around 1,350 firms. Capturing 1% of that reachable set is 13 clients. At a blended £400/month that is **£62,400 a year recurring**, before any project work.

**UK reception plus automation, year two.** 25 reception clients at £400 blended is £120,000 recurring. If half also buy one automation build a year at £3,000, that is another £37,500. **Around £157,000 annually**, achievable with two people if the sales rhythm holds.

**Adding Eastern Province Saudi.** Deal sizes there run larger because self-hosted deployment plus PDPL compliance work is a project, not a subscription. Three to five Saudi clients a year at £15,000–30,000 initial plus a support retainer would roughly **double the UK figure**, and this is the realistic path to a business worth more than a good salary.

**What would make it genuinely valuable.** Recurring revenue is what gets valued; project work is what pays the bills while recurring grows. A business at £250,000 ARR with low churn and a defensible niche is a real asset. Getting there needs roughly 50 recurring clients or a smaller number of larger self-hosted contracts. On the cashflow model's default ramp that is a three to four year build, not a one year one.

## The honest risks

**Focus is the biggest one, by some distance.** Four service lines across three countries, with zero clients, is not a strategy. It is four half-built offers. The market data supports a much narrower start: UK legal reception, sold locally in east London and Essex, with automation as the natural second sale into the same clients. Saudi is a genuine second market but only through Hammad's Eastern Province network, and only once you have a working reference client.

**Consolidation cuts both ways.** 519 law firms closed against 346 opened in the last year, and advice firms fell 15% since 2021. Your buyer pool is shrinking slightly, and some of your clients will be acquired. Fewer, larger buyers means longer sales cycles and more procurement.

**The 12% problem.** Only 12% of AI-using UK businesses report increased revenue from it. If your clients cannot point to money, they will not renew. This is why tying your reporting to their revenue event, rather than to calls answered, is not a marketing choice but a retention requirement.

**You are 18 with no clients and no case study.** The leadership section exists to offset this, and it does real work. But no amount of website will substitute for one solicitor in Romford saying yes. Everything in this document is downstream of that.

## What I would actually do next

1. Build a list of every law firm within a 45-minute journey of Romford. Somewhere between 40 and 80 firms.
2. Filter to 5–20 fee earners: big enough to have the problem, small enough to have no IT department and a decision-maker you can reach.
3. Walk in. Ask for the practice manager. Ask one question: how many enquiries do you think you miss outside office hours?
4. Do not pitch on the first visit. Come back with a demo built on their actual practice areas.
5. In parallel, ask Hammad for three warm introductions in Dammam or Khobar, and take one Saudi discovery call before building anything for that market.

The research says the demand exists, the buyers have money this year, and the compliance angle is real. None of that matters until somebody signs.
